Predator & Prey

For about two centuries, the Hudson's Bay Company kept records of the furs trappers brought in from northern Canada. Ecologists later noticed that the numbers of snowshoe hare and lynx pelts rose and fell in a cycle of about ten years. Hares boomed, then the lynx that ate them boomed, then the hares crashed, then the lynx starved, and around it went.

In the 1920s, Alfred Lotka and Vito Volterra, working separately, wrote down a pair of simple equations that produce exactly that kind of endless chase. It's one of the classic ideas of mathematical biology.

What's happening on the field

  • Grass is eaten down to bare earth and slowly grows back.
  • Rabbits wander and graze. A well-fed rabbit has babies; a hungry one starves. They run from foxes they can see.
  • Foxes chase rabbits they can see. A good meal lets them raise cubs; a fox that can't catch anything starves.

Nobody tells the populations to rise and fall. Watch the chart at the top: rabbits peak, foxes peak a little later, then both crash. The little square in the corner plots rabbits against foxes, and the cycle traces out a loop.

Things to try

  • Scenarios: "Rabbits alone" shows that grass alone sets a limit on rabbits. "Too many foxes" shows predators crashing first. "Feast and famine" gives the wildest swings.
  • + Rabbits / + Foxes: switch one on, then tap the field to drop animals in. Tap the button again to stop.
  • Settings: change the starting numbers, how fast grass grows back, and how hungry foxes get.

If a species ever dies out completely, a few newcomers wander in from outside the field after a while, just as real animals would, and a note tells you so.
